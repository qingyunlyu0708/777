## 5.1 Discussion

Read together, the three sets of findings describe a single mechanism. Users enter the
platform and co-create value through four interaction activities, exploration, creation,
socialising and governance, which generate experiential and aesthetic, content and symbolic,
relational and emotional, and normative and institutional value respectively. Repeated
participation in these activities settles into five co-creator roles that form a developmental
trajectory, as experiential participants become prosumers, organisers and mentors, and as
some, in time, become critics. Each role is sustained only by a continuing investment of
resources, and it is exactly these resources that the five structural barriers withdraw.
Operating not in parallel but as a recursive sequence across the participation journey, from
access constraints and fragile infrastructure, through relational erosion and normative
breakdown, to perceived platform unresponsiveness, they progressively dismantle the conditions
that hold the trajectory in place. Co-creation is therefore not destroyed at a stroke but
attenuated over time.

What this means is that the same depth of engagement that produces value also produces
vulnerability. Because users invest worlds, relationships and identity in the platform, they
have the most to lose when that investment is left unprotected, and two patterns make the point
concrete. The most committed contributors, once governance fails them, do not leave quietly but
become the platform's most influential critics, shaping its reputation and steering others
towards exit; and the relational warmth that draws and holds users rests on the sustained,
unpaid emotional labour of organisers and mentors, which turns to exhaustion once technical and
governance support falls away.

The significance of this account is that it treats the bright and dark sides of online value
co-creation as one process rather than two. An immersive community does not simply accumulate
value or, separately, suffer failures; it builds co-creative vitality and then, under
accumulating and unaddressed friction, loses it along the very trajectory that created it. The
remainder of this section draws out what this means for theory and for practice.

## 5.2 Theoretical implications

These findings yield three theoretical contributions. First, we extend value co-creation theory
into immersive, spatially embodied environments. Where co-creation has largely been theorised
through screen-based practices, we show that it can be place-based and avatar-mediated,
integrating embodied presence and attachment to virtual space as resources in their own right
(Belk, 2013; Vargo and Lusch, 2008). This specifies, rather than merely asserts, the
service-dominant claim that value is co-created in context.

Second, we conceptualise platform-mediated co-creation as a layered interaction architecture.
The value a platform sustains arises not from any single user but from a division of labour in
which distinct roles supply attention, content, traffic and cohesion, on-boarding and
governance signalling, and in which users move between those roles along an engagement
trajectory. This reframes co-creator roles from a typology into a functional architecture and
connects role research to the resource-integration premise of service-dominant logic
(Kolyperas et al., 2019; Lave and Wenger, 1991).

Third, and most centrally, we introduce co-creation attenuation as a construct distinct from
value co-destruction. Attenuation names a gradual, cumulative and recursive erosion of
co-creation that operates at the ecosystem level, in which structural barriers compound across
the participation journey and committed users either withdraw or convert into critics. It is
best understood as an antecedent to, and a softer precursor of, co-destruction, and it
specifies a mechanism rather than an outcome, offering a vocabulary for the slow decline of
online value co-creation that disengagement, fatigue and platform decay do not fully capture
(Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Brodie et al., 2011). The committed-to-critic conversion and
the exhaustion of emotional labour are the principal sub-mechanisms of this process, and
together they qualify the assumption in engagement research that deeper engagement is uniformly
beneficial.

Cutting across the roles, the recursive nature of attenuation implies that platform
responsiveness is the single highest-leverage intervention. Because perceived unresponsiveness
is the barrier that converts dissatisfaction into public criticism and exit, visibly acting on
