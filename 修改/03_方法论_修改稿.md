## 4.1 Research design and context

This study adopts a qualitative, abductive research design (Dubois and Gadde, 2002) that
combines immersive netnography with semi-structured interviews to examine how value is
co-created and attenuated in a social virtual world (SVW). An abductive logic is appropriate
because the analysis moves iteratively between empirical observation and theoretical
concepts, allowing the four activity clusters, five co-creator roles and five structural
barriers to emerge from the data while being progressively refined against value co-creation
theory. The empirical context is Light World (version 1.6.3), an SVW launched by PICO in
November 2022 and accessed through PICO VR headsets. Represented by avatars, users customise
their appearance, explore and build persistent user-generated worlds, and interact through
real-time spatial voice and gesture, with each world hosting roughly 10 to 16 co-present
users and thereby forming micro-communities. As established in Section 2.5, Light World is a
VR-based SVW rather than a full metaverse; some user-built worlds resemble shops, galleries
or plazas, but we treat these as creative and social settings and make no claim about
commercial transactions. Combining the two methods lets naturally occurring practices,
captured through netnography, be triangulated with participants' own interpretations,
captured through interviews.

## 4.2 Netnographic observation

Observation followed immersive netnographic protocols developed for VR and metaverse settings
(Kozinets, 2015, 2023), in which the researcher participates in, rather than passively
watches, the community. Observation was confined to publicly accessible worlds and public
chat. We selected worlds to capture the breadth of the community: high-traffic social hubs and
regularly scheduled community events, alongside quieter creator-built worlds reached through
in-world navigation and snowball referrals from contacts. Fieldwork was conducted between
January and June 2026 and totalled approximately 90 hours, distributed across different days
of the week and times of day so as not to over-represent any single activity rhythm. The
first author participated as an engaged member under a consistent, recognisable avatar, and
disclosed the research purpose to participants with whom sustained interaction or interviews
took place. Three iterative phases structured the fieldwork: (1) familiarisation with platform
affordances, navigation and user-generated worlds; (2) active engagement with the Light
Workshop creation toolkit, collaborative creation and social interaction; and (3) focused
observation of structural disruptions, user complaints, exit behaviours and norm violations.
The fieldwork yielded 110 screenshots, 457 manually transcribed public chat logs and an
8,000-word reflexive field diary.

## 4.3 Semi-structured interviews

To complement observation and, crucially, to capture the full participation spectrum, we used
maximum-variation purposive sampling (Patton, 2015). Ten participants were recruited through
in-world invitations and snowball referrals, deliberately spanning newcomers and
low-frequency users, regular and highly engaged contributors, and users whose participation
had markedly declined or who had already exited the platform; the latter were reached through
contacts retained from the fieldwork. Sampling for this range directly addresses the risk that
highly engaged insiders alone would bias an account of attenuation and exit, since users who
withdraw are precisely those whose perspective that account requires. A pilot interview was
used to refine the protocol. Interviews lasted 30 to 45 minutes and were conducted through
in-app or other voice communication, covering (1) typical activities and interaction
patterns; (2) perceived roles and contributions to value co-creation; and (3) experienced
challenges, barriers and processes of decline or disengagement. All interviews were
audio-recorded with informed consent and transcribed verbatim. Participant characteristics
are summarised in Appendix A.

## 4.4 Data analysis

Data analysis followed an abductive, multi-stage coding procedure emphasising constant
comparison between empirical material and theoretical concepts (Dubois and Gadde, 2002; Gioia
et al., 2013). All screenshots, field-diary notes, chat logs and interview transcripts were
consolidated into a single database. In the first cycle, the material was open-coded into
first-order codes that stayed close to participants' own terms (for example, world touring,
avatar customisation, hosting events, feeling welcomed, feeling excluded, reporting bugs,
threatening to quit). In the second cycle, these codes were clustered through constant
comparison into second-order themes, namely the four interaction-activity clusters, the five
co-creator roles and the five structural barriers, which were then organised into the
study's aggregate dimensions of interaction activities, co-creator roles, structural barriers
and co-creation attenuation. This structure was built from the data upward rather than imposed
from theory, and coding continued until additional material generated no new categories,
indicating thematic saturation. Coding was performed by the first author, and the developing
codes, categories and interpretations were subjected to regular peer debriefing with the
supervisory team, whose challenges were used to question, stabilise and refine the scheme.
Participant persona profiles were maintained to cross-validate emerging themes by linking each
co-creator type to its activities, interactions and co-created value (Figure 2).

The two data sources were triangulated throughout: netnographic material established what
users did in situ, while interviews clarified why, and points of convergence and divergence
between them were treated as occasions for further analysis rather than smoothed over. To make
the evidentiary status of each excerpt explicit, observational material is labelled N and
interview material I, with publicly observed verbatim chat presented in quotation marks and
distinguished from paraphrased field-diary observations, and the researcher's interpretation
kept separate from the data it interprets. Table 1 illustrates the trail from representative
quotes to first-order codes and higher-order themes.

## 4.5 Trustworthiness and ethics

Trustworthiness was addressed against the criteria of Lincoln and Guba (1985). Credibility was
supported by prolonged engagement of approximately 90 hours, triangulation of observation and
interviews, peer debriefing with the supervisory team, and deliberate attention to negative
cases that did not fit emerging categories. Transferability was supported by thick description
and the explicit boundary statement of the case (Section 2.5), and dependability and
confirmability by the audit trail described above and a reflexive field diary documenting the
researcher's evolving position. Because the data were generated in Chinese, the first author
translated the cited excerpts into English and, to protect participants, lightly paraphrased
them in ways that preserved meaning while reducing traceability; we acknowledge reliance on a
single translator as a limitation.

The study received institutional ethical approval prior to data collection. Observation was
limited to publicly accessible worlds and public chat, and interview participants provided
informed consent for recording and use of their accounts. All avatar names, world names and
other potentially identifying details were anonymised, and screenshots were edited to remove
identifying information. Abusive or potentially harmful speech is reported only where it is
analytically necessary and is presented as a participant's framing rather than the authors'
language. No personal data identifying minors were collected.
