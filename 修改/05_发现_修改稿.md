Our analysis traces a single arc: how interaction activities create value (RQ1), how they
configure users into co-creator roles (RQ2), and how structural barriers progressively
attenuate co-creation across those roles (RQ3). Read through S-D logic, each practice
integrates particular user resources with particular platform affordances to yield a distinct
form of value (Vargo and Lusch, 2008). N marks observation, I interviews.

## 5.1 Interaction activities

Co-creation begins in embodied exploration. Wandering and dwelling in immersive, user-built
worlds generate hedonic and aesthetic value beyond any utilitarian purpose (Hirschman and
Holbrook, 1982). When a heavy user calls Light World "the home of my soul" (N01) and a shelter
where, when "reality is dull", the world "feels warm" (N25), the language is not mere
enthusiasm but affective place attachment and a symbolic extension of the self into the world
(Belk, 2013): time, attention and aesthetic sensibility are integrated with the platform's
persistent, VR-rendered space. Crucially, this immersion is generative, as dwelling turns into
the creative question, "if it were me, how would I design this world?".

That question pulls users from consuming worlds to producing them. In prosumption, creative
labour combines with the creation toolkit to author both content and identity (Ritzer and
Jurgenson, 2010; Schau et al., 2009). Driven partly by scarcity, "if we want more fun content,
we must make it ourselves" (N10), creators build the worlds others socialise and learn within,
the study's content value. The same labour reauthors the self: a self-described "piano teacher
with a boring life" becomes a creator whose "updates are followed and design services are
requested" (I03), converting recognition into symbolic value (Belk, 2013). Because creators
invite others to visit and test, their worlds become social nodes.

In those nodes a third form of value accrues. Hosting and joining gatherings, "set[ting] a
time for everyone to come in together" (I01), builds companionship and belonging (Algesheimer
et al., 2005). The mechanism is clearest at the margins of sociability: a user "quite socially
anxious" offline nonetheless "frequently initiates chats" through avatar gestures (N12),
because avatar mediation and embodied co-presence lower the social risk of interaction,
integrating social ties and emotional labour with spatial voice to co-create relational value
that is hard to obtain elsewhere.

As relationships deepen, users grow invested in protecting the space and begin to act as its
citizens (Yi and Gong, 2013). Their recurrent target is map bombing, a disruptive practice in
which users deploy high-load objects to crash a shared world and force others out. Demanding
that "the officials delete those props" (N13), users integrate platform knowledge and
normative expectation with public chat to co-create normative and institutional value: order,
safety and trust. Yet this work is double-edged. Once "report is no use" (I04), the same civic
energy turns into criticism and exit. Performed repeatedly and with different emphases, the
four activities do not stay evenly spread; they harden into roles.

## 5.2 Value co-creator roles

If activities are what users do, roles are who they become. We distinguish five roles not by
attitude or sheer intensity but by the dominant resource each contributes, and we treat them
not as fixed types but as positions along a developmental trajectory: users typically enter at
the periphery, some move inward as their investment grows, and a committed minority, when that
investment is not protected, move outward again as critics (Lave and Wenger, 1991;
McColl-Kennedy et al., 2012).

At the entry point are experiential participants, who mainly explore and lightly socialise,
"window-shop[ping]" worlds and "casually comment[ing] on scenes" (N25). Though they create
little, they are the demand-side backbone: their attention and movement signal which worlds
are worth visiting, making it worthwhile for others to build and host. Those who move inward
become creation-oriented prosumers, the productive core. Recalling "maybe I can try this too"
before "learning the editor tools" (I03), they supply the worlds others inhabit, a
contribution that is generative twice over, producing both content and the spatial
infrastructure on which the next roles depend, which positions them as the hinge of the
ecosystem.

Around that infrastructure, social organisers do the work of assembly. One explained, "I'm not
the best creator, but I'm good at gathering people and making a night feel special" (I01). By
scheduling gatherings and channelling attention back to creators, they generate traffic and
repeat visitation. This coordination is sustained, however, by considerable unpaid emotional
labour, the welcoming and atmosphere-keeping that holds a gathering together (Hochschild,
1983), and it therefore depends on stability: as map bombing and "declining user numbers"
mount, organisers' energy is the first to erode. Knowledge mentors perform a parallel,
equally affective service. Describing themselves as "helpers" who "teach … step by step" until
a newcomer "finish[es] their first work" (I03), they lower the entry barriers that would
otherwise confine creation to the technically skilled, expanding the productive base. Like
organisers, mentors give time and care with little formal reward, so that the community's
onboarding rests on voluntary emotional labour, robust while the community thrives but quick
to exhaust when conditions deteriorate.

The trajectory's most consequential turn is its reversal. Critics and negotiators are, for the
most part, not disaffected outsiders but former prosumers and organisers, the platform's most
invested members, whose commitment has curdled after repeated, unaddressed problems. One
announced he would "play one last day and then never come back" (I05); another, that Light
World is "basically unplayable now" (I04). This inversion is theoretically central: because
their judgements carry the authority of heavy investment, committed users turning critic do
not leave quietly but shape newcomers' perceptions and warn others away, so that the same depth
of engagement that once anchored value now accelerates its erosion. Engagement is thus at once
the ecosystem's main source of value and its main source of vulnerability. Read together, the
five roles describe one arc, from peripheral experience through creative, social and pedagogic
investment to critical withdrawal, in which each role sustains the next only so long as its
contribution is supported. What withdraws that support is the set of structural barriers we
examine next.

## 5.3 Structural barriers

These barriers are not parallel obstacles but a recursive sequence, each weakening the
conditions on which the next role depends and, cumulatively, driving co-creation attenuation
(Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Echeverri and Skålén, 2021). It begins at the threshold.
Access and participation constraints, device incompatibility and a toolkit whose "editing
logic with VR is too painful" without "a PC or web editor" (N43), confine production to a
narrow group of skilled prosumers, thinning the creator base before co-creation can broaden.
For those who persist, fragile interaction infrastructure devalues their effort: when logic
"works fine in trial but break[s] in the interface" (N38), accumulated creative labour feels
wasted, discouraging the ambitious projects on which content depends.

As content thins and instability persists, relational and emotional erosion follows. The
recurring lament that "nobody plays anymore" and that "many quality creators are quitting"
(N26) marks the draining of the audiences and collaborators that gave organisers and mentors
their reason to continue, reversing the relational value built earlier. Into the emptying
space, normative breakdown spreads, as verbal abuse and map bombing make public worlds
hostile. One participant's dismissal of disruptive users as "socially awkward losers" (N36),
which we report as a member's hostile framing rather than our own, captures how toxicity raises
the emotional cost of being present, pushing experiential participants and organisers into
private instances and shrinking the shared space of co-creation.

What converts these frictions into exit is perceived platform unresponsiveness. As reports and
suggestions yield no visible change and the platform feels "forgotten and abandoned" (N103),
trust collapses. This is the amplifying barrier: the committed users of the previous section,
having exhausted constructive voice, adopt the critic's stance and discourage others from
investing, so that disappointment becomes contagious. No single failure ends co-creation.
Rather, access narrows the creator base, fragile infrastructure frustrates those who remain,
depopulation erodes relationships, toxicity raises the cost of presence, and unresponsiveness
destroys trust, each layer compounding the last. This cumulative, structural drift, rather
than any abrupt breakdown, is what we term co-creation attenuation, the mechanism we theorise
next.
