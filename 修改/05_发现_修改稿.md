Our analysis is organised around the causal chain set out earlier. We first identify the
interaction activities through which users co-create value and the value each generates
(RQ1); we then show how repeated activities configure users into distinct co-creator roles
(RQ2); and finally how structural barriers progressively attenuate co-creation across those
roles (RQ3). Throughout, we read each practice through S-D logic, specifying which resources
users integrate, through which platform affordances, to produce which form of value (Vargo
and Lusch, 2008). Observational excerpts are marked N and interview excerpts I.

## 5.1 Interaction activities

Four activity clusters emerged, each integrating particular user resources with particular
platform affordances to produce a distinct form of co-created value (Appendix B, Table B1).

### 5.1.1 Exploratory and experiential activities

Value co-creation in the SVW begins with embodied exploration. Consistent with experiential
consumption theory, immersive wandering and dwelling generate hedonic and aesthetic value
that exceeds any utilitarian purpose (Hirschman and Holbrook, 1982; Schau et al., 2009). A
heavy user recalled:

"I still remember the first time I entered Light World; it was a digital art world, and every
exploration revealed a fresh and wonderful place … it has become the home of my soul." (N01)

The phrase "the home of my soul" is more than enthusiasm. It signals affective place
attachment and a symbolic extension of the self into the virtual world (Belk, 2013): the user
integrates time, attention and aesthetic sensibility with the platform's spatially persistent,
VR-rendered worlds, and the value so produced is experiential and aesthetic rather than
transactional. This immersion is also generative. As users dwell, passive appreciation gives
way to creative intent, captured in the recurring question, "if it were me, how would I design
this world?", which seeds the creative activities discussed next.

### 5.1.2 Creative and collaborative activities

A second cluster moves users from consuming worlds to producing them. In prosumption,
participants integrate creative labour with platform tools to generate user-generated content
and, in doing so, to author a valued identity (Ritzer and Jurgenson, 2010; Schau et al.,
2009). One creator described the shift:

"At first I was wandering around; after seeing other people's worlds I thought, 'maybe I can
try this too.' Then I started learning the editor tools. When I saw players entering my worlds
and enjoying them, that sense of achievement was huge." (I03)

Two forms of value are co-created here. The worlds and props themselves are content value: as
the same creator noted, content scarcity drives production ("if we want more fun content, we
must make it ourselves", N10), and these creations become the infrastructure on which others
socialise and learn. At the same time, creation yields symbolic value: the creator who was "a
piano teacher with a boring life" became someone whose "updates are followed and design
services are requested", an identity transformation through recognition (Belk, 2013).
Productively, users integrate skill, creativity and time with the Light Workshop toolkit and
avatar system. Because creators invite others to visit and test their worlds, creative spaces
become nodes of social interaction, leading into the third cluster.

### 5.1.3 Social and emotional activities

A third cluster centres on building and sustaining relationships, producing relational and
emotional value through companionship, belonging and social support (Algesheimer et al., 2005;
Ridings and Gefen, 2004). An organiser described the rhythm of community life:

"Back then we started organising regular events in specific worlds … on festival days we would
choose a themed plaza, decorate it, and set a time for everyone to come in together." (I01)

This value is especially salient for users for whom face-to-face interaction is difficult. One
participant who was "quite socially anxious" and "barely meet[s] new people" in daily life
nonetheless "frequently initiates chats and uses avatar gestures to express friendliness" in
Light World (N12). The interpretation matters: avatar mediation and embodied co-presence lower
the social risk of interaction, so that users can integrate social ties and emotional labour
with the platform's spatial voice and gesture affordances to co-create relational value that
some find hard to obtain offline. As these ties deepen, users become invested not only in one
another but in the conditions that protect their shared space, which motivates the fourth
cluster.

### 5.1.4 Governance and negotiation activities

A fourth cluster arises when accumulated relational and emotional investment leads users to
act as community citizens, articulating norms and demanding responsiveness (Yi and Gong, 2013;
Kraut and Resnick, 2012). A frequent target of these demands is map bombing, which we define
as a disruptive practice in which users deploy high-load virtual objects to overload and crash
a shared world, forcing co-present users to leave. In public chat, users press the platform to
act:

"It's a shame Light World is ruined by people bombing maps every day. Just when a map finally
gets lively, someone uses bombing props to drive everyone away. Why don't the officials delete
those props?" (N13)

Such appeals co-create normative and institutional value: by naming violations and negotiating
rules, users integrate their platform knowledge and normative expectations with public chat
and reporting affordances to produce, when heeded, a measure of order, safety and trust. Yet
this activity is double-edged. When demands go unmet, as when a user concludes that "report is
no use" (I04), the same governance energy curdles into public criticism and withdrawal, a
dynamic we develop in the analysis of barriers and attenuation.

Taken together, the four clusters form an interconnected circuit: exploration seeds creation,
creation concentrates social interaction, deepening ties motivate governance, and governance
outcomes reshape the conditions for exploration, creation and socialising. Performed
repeatedly, and with different resource emphases, these activities do not remain evenly
distributed across users; they crystallise into recognisable co-creator roles, to which we now
turn.
