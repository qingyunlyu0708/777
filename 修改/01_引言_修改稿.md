## Introduction

Over the past two decades, value co-creation has become a dominant lens for explaining
how users generate value in online environments. Grounded in service-dominant (S-D) logic,
this perspective recasts users from passive recipients into resource integrators who
co-create value through their participation, interaction and content contribution
(Vargo and Lusch, 2004, 2008). A large body of work on online communities and social
platforms has shown that practices such as sharing, socialising, helping and collaborative
creation generate experiential, relational and informational value for users, peers and
platforms alike (Schau et al., 2009; Tajvidi et al., 2021). Implicit in much of this work
is an optimistic and broadly cumulative assumption: that deeper participation and richer
resource integration progressively enhance value, drawing users from the periphery toward
the centre of the community.

This optimistic account, however, sits uneasily with mounting evidence that participation
can also erode value. Research on value co-destruction (Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010;
Echeverri and Skålén, 2011), the dark side of engagement (Heidenreich et al., 2015), and
platform fatigue and disengagement (Nadeem et al., 2025b) documents how interaction can
deplete rather than create well-being. Yet these negative dynamics are typically theorised
as discrete failures, isolated episodes or distinct phenomena, conceptually separated from
the value-creating processes that precede them. As a result, we know comparatively little
about how value creation and its erosion are connected within the same trajectory of
participation — how the very engagement that generates value may, over time, also generate
the conditions for its decline. This tension — between co-creation as a virtuous, cumulative
process and as a fragile, reversible one — is the point of departure for this study.

This tension becomes especially acute, and especially visible, in immersive,
avatar-mediated virtual environments. Unlike the text- and screen-based communities on which
most co-creation research is built (Schau et al., 2009), persistent social VR platforms
involve embodied presence, spatial co-location and real-time voice, such that participation
is more immersive, more effortful and more emotionally invested (Belk, 2013; Kozinets, 2023).
The same properties that deepen engagement and value may also heighten users' exposure to
technical fragility, social friction and governance failure. Despite the rapid growth of
these environments, we still lack a grounded understanding of three connected issues: what
users actually do to create value, how their participation differentiates into distinct
co-creator roles, and how — and why — co-creation erodes over time. Existing role
typologies, developed largely in screen-based travel, sports and brand communities
(Kolyperas et al., 2019; Chapman and Dilmperi, 2022; Xu et al., 2025), offer limited
purchase on the embodied, spatially organised participation of immersive platforms, and
rarely connect role dynamics to the processes through which engagement decays.

These observations point to a specific empirical puzzle. In the platform we study, it was
not the casual or peripheral users but the most deeply invested co-creators — the builders,
hosts and mentors who had contributed the most value — who became the platform's most vocal
critics and, ultimately, among the first to leave. Why do a platform's most valuable
participants become an engine of its decline? Addressing this puzzle requires treating
engagement not only as a source of value but also as a source of vulnerability, and tracing
the process through which the two become bound together.

To do so, we ground our study in S-D logic and treat value co-creation as a processual
sequence rather than a static state. We pose three research questions that build on one
another. Because value originates in what users do, we first ask:

- **RQ1: What interaction activities do users engage in, and what forms of value do these
  activities create?**

Because such activities are not undertaken uniformly but become differentiated as users
invest different resources over time, we then ask:

- **RQ2: How do these activities crystallise into distinct value co-creator roles?**

And because roles must be sustained by ongoing resource investment that platform conditions
can either support or undermine, we finally ask:

- **RQ3: How do structural barriers progressively attenuate co-creation across these roles?**

Together, these questions trace a single causal chain — from activities, to the value they
create, to the roles they configure, to the structural conditions that sustain or erode
those roles over time — moving the analysis from classification toward explanation.

We address these questions through a qualitative, abductive study combining immersive
netnography and semi-structured interviews on Light World, a persistent social VR platform
accessed through PICO VR headsets, in which users explore, build and inhabit user-generated
worlds together.

Our analysis traces a developmental trajectory that lies at the heart of the paper. Users
typically enter as experiential participants engaged in peripheral exploration; some move
into deeper creative and social involvement as builders, organisers and mentors; and a
subset, under the weight of accumulated frustration, transform into critics and negotiators
before withdrawing altogether. This trajectory shows how participation operates
simultaneously as a source of value and a source of vulnerability. We theorise its downward
phase as **co-creation attenuation**: a gradual, cumulative erosion of co-creation —
distinct from abrupt value co-destruction — produced not by a single breakdown but by the
compounding misalignment of access, infrastructure, relational, normative and governance
conditions. In doing so, the study makes three contributions: it extends value co-creation
research into embodied, persistent virtual environments; it reconceptualises online
co-creator roles as an evolving trajectory rather than a static typology; and it introduces
co-creation attenuation as a processual account of how engagement-driven value declines,
contributing to research on online communities, user participation and the dark side of
engagement.
