1. Introduction

Over the past two decades, the locus of value has shifted markedly towards the interaction and co-creation that take place among users, platforms and other actors. Service-dominant (S-D) logic recasts users as active resource integrators and co-creators rather than passive recipients of pre-produced value, holding that value is realised in use and in emergent contexts (Vargo and Lusch, 2004, 2008). Online platforms have become the principal arena for this shift: users browse, share, create and transact, integrating their own resources of time, skills, emotions and relationships with the platform to co-create experiential, relational and informational value (Prahalad and Ramaswamy, 2004; Schau et al., 2009; Hajli et al., 2017). Understanding how such co-creation is organised has become central to research on online communities, user-generated content and digital information environments, much of which rests on an optimistic, cumulative assumption: that deeper participation and richer resource integration progressively enhance value, drawing users from the periphery towards the centre of the community.

This account sits uneasily with mounting evidence that participation can also erode value. Studies of value co-destruction (Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Echeverri and Skålén, 2011), the dark side of engagement (Heidenreich et al., 2015) and platform fatigue (Nadeem et al., 2025b) document how interaction may deplete rather than create well-being. Yet these dynamics are typically theorised as discrete failures or separate phenomena, detached from the value-creating processes that precede them. Comparatively little is known about how value creation and its erosion are linked within a single trajectory of participation, that is, how the very engagement that generates value may, over time, also produce the conditions for its decline. This tension, between co-creation as a virtuous, cumulative process and as a fragile, reversible one, is the point of departure for this study.

This tension is not merely more visible in immersive, avatar-mediated platforms; it is intensified by them. Unlike the text- and screen-based communities on which most co-creation research is built (Schau et al., 2009), embodied social VR platforms involve bodily presence, spatial co-location and real-time voice, so that participation is more immersive, more effortful and more emotionally invested (Belk, 2013; Kozinets, 2023). These properties make such platforms powerful engines of co-creation, yet they also intensify its decline. High immersion magnifies the felt cost of latency, instability and harmful behaviour; deep relational investment raises the stakes when technical conditions fail to protect what users have committed; and when governance fails, those who have contributed most may become a platform's most destructive critics. Immersion therefore does not simply reveal the fragility of co-creation but actively amplifies it.

Brought to this setting, existing knowledge proves inadequate in three respects, and tracing these limits brings the gap into focus. First, accounts of how co-creation practices are built in such communities (Tajvidi et al., 2021) say little about how embodied, spatial interaction reorganises them. Second, typologies of co-creator roles, established in screen-based sports and travel communities (Kolyperas et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2025), have not been examined where identity is enacted through avatars and self-built worlds, leaving both role classification and role transition unclear. Third, the negative side of co-creation has largely been theorised as value co-destruction, namely discrete, identifiable episodes of diminished well-being (Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Echeverri and Skålén, 2021), a framing that captures sudden failures but overlooks the slow, structural erosion through which participation quietly declines. Together these converge on one sharper gap: an integrated account of how embodied interaction organises co-creation, configures evolving roles and is progressively weakened over time remains lacking.

To address this gap, we ground the study in S-D logic and treat value co-creation as a dynamic, processual sequence rather than a static state, posing three interrelated research questions:

RQ1. Through what interaction activities do users co-create value on an immersive social platform, and what forms of value do these activities generate?

RQ2. How do these activities configure different users into distinct co-creator roles?

RQ3. How do structural barriers progressively attenuate co-creation across these roles and over time?

The logic linking the three is cumulative: interaction activities create different forms of value; repeated participation crystallises these into roles sustained by continued resource investment; and as structural barriers withdraw the conditions supporting those roles, the roles and the co-creation they sustain gradually unravel.

We address these questions through a qualitative, abductive study combining netnography and semi-structured interviews on Light World, a social VR platform accessed through PICO VR headsets, in which avatar-represented users inhabit persistent, user-generated worlds and gather in shared public spaces through spatial audio and gesture.

Our analysis traces a developmental trajectory that constitutes the paper's central narrative. Users typically enter as experiential participants engaged in peripheral exploration; some move into deeper creative and social involvement as builders, organisers and mentors; and a subset, under accumulated frustration, become critics and negotiators before withdrawing altogether. Participation is thus at once a source of value and a source of vulnerability. We theorise its declining phase as co-creation attenuation, a gradual, cumulative erosion of co-creation that, unlike abrupt value co-destruction, arises not from a single breakdown but from the compounding misalignment of access, infrastructure, relational, normative and governance conditions. The study offers three contributions. First, it extends value co-creation research into embodied, persistent virtual environments. Second, it reconceptualises online co-creator roles as an evolving trajectory rather than a static typology. Third, it introduces co-creation attenuation as a processual account of how engagement-driven value declines, contributing to research on online communities, user participation and the dark side of engagement.
