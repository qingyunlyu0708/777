## 2.1 Value co-creation

Service-dominant (S-D) logic provides the premise for this study: value is not embedded in
outputs but co-created in use and in context by actors who integrate their resources (Vargo
and Lusch, 2004, 2008). Rather than rehearsing this well-established position, we foreground
the mechanism that matters for online environments. Value emerges when actors integrate
operant resources, such as skills, knowledge, social ties and creativity, and operand
resources, such as tools, content and time, through the affordances a platform provides,
within institutional arrangements that coordinate their interaction (Lusch and Vargo, 2014;
Vargo et al., 2015; Ng and Vargo, 2018). Process accounts add that co-creation unfolds over
time through reciprocal encounters rather than single transactions (Payne et al., 2008;
Grönroos, 2008).

We therefore adopt a compositional view of co-creation: a given value outcome is produced
when specific actors integrate specific resources through specific platform affordances.
Stating the mechanism this way moves the analysis beyond the broad claim that users are
resource integrators, and lets us ask which resources, through which affordances and by which
actors generate which forms of value (Pan, 2020). In an online setting the resources at issue
are principally those of users, who contribute skills, creativity, social ties, emotional
labour, time and attention, and those of the platform, which supplies spatial infrastructure,
creation tools, avatar systems, real-time communication and governance mechanisms. The value
such integration yields is not singular, however, which first requires distinguishing the
forms that co-creation produces.

## 2.2 Forms of co-created value

Because co-creation generates heterogeneous outcomes, the value it produces must be
distinguished analytically rather than treated as a single undifferentiated good (Saarijärvi
et al., 2013; Alves et al., 2016). Synthesising work on online communities and digital
participation, we distinguish four recurring forms that structure the later analysis.
Experiential and aesthetic value is the immersive, pleasurable and escapist worth of moving
through and dwelling in online spaces (Schau et al., 2009). Content and symbolic value is
generated through creative production: it spans the user-generated content and knowledge that
creation circulates and the identity, self-expression and recognition that creators derive
from it (Muniz and O'Guinn, 2001; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Belk, 2013; Estrella-Ramón and
Ellis-Chadwick, 2017). Relational and emotional value arises from companionship, social
support and belonging built through community ties (Algesheimer et al., 2005; Ridings and
Gefen, 2004). Normative and institutional value is the order, safety and trust produced when
users help define and enforce community rules (Kraut and Resnick, 2012). Defining these forms
in advance allows the findings to show, cumulatively, how each cluster of interaction
activities generates a corresponding form of value. Yet these forms are not produced
uniformly across users, which turns attention to the roles participants come to occupy.

## 2.3 Co-creator roles

A long tradition in online community research shows that participation is unevenly
distributed and role-differentiated. Members range from silent lurkers to active posters and
core contributors, and the boundary between them is permeable (Lave and Wenger, 1991;
Nonnecke and Preece, 2000; Preece et al., 2004; Sun et al., 2014). Whether members
contribute, and how far they move from the periphery towards the centre, depends on the
resources and incentives at play, including reputation, reciprocity, social capital and a
sense of community (Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Chiu et al., 2006; Blanchard and Markus, 2004;
Ridings and Gefen, 2004; Hsu et al., 2012). Consumer-engagement research similarly frames
engagement as a dynamic, multidimensional state that intensifies and recedes over time
(Brodie et al., 2011, 2013; Dessart et al., 2015).

Value co-creation research has translated these patterns into role typologies: assimilators,
adaptors and authenticators in sport fandom (Kolyperas et al., 2019); diverse contributor
types on travel platforms (Xu et al., 2025); and the movement of passive lurkers into active
posting in brand communities (Chapman and Dilmperi, 2022; McColl-Kennedy et al., 2012). Two
limitations recur across this work. First, the classification logic is often mixed, combining
activity, motivation and intensity, so that the basis on which roles are distinguished, and
the mechanisms by which users move between them, remain unclear. Second, and more
consequentially, the work is largely developed in screen-based settings and emphasises the
upward path from periphery to centre, paying little attention to how identity is enacted
through avatars and self-built worlds, or to the reverse movement in which central
contributors disengage and exit. Existing typologies thus treat roles largely as a static
classification, leaving open how a single trajectory might carry users from peripheral
participation, through deep contribution, and ultimately towards withdrawal, a downward
movement that existing accounts of co-creation rarely theorise.

## 2.4 From co-destruction to attenuation

A growing body of work tempers the optimistic view of co-creation by examining its negative
counterpart. Value co-destruction refers to interactions in which actors' resource
integration diminishes rather than enhances well-being, typically arising from the
misintegration of resources or misaligned practices rather than a single catastrophic failure
(Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Echeverri and Skålén, 2011, 2021). Related streams document
the dark side of engagement and failed co-creation (Heidenreich et al., 2015), the
disinhibition and toxic behaviour that corrode online interaction (Suler, 2004; Cheng et al.,
2017), and the fatigue, overload and exhaustion that drive disengagement and discontinuance
from online platforms (Ravindran et al., 2014; Maier et al., 2015; Nadeem et al., 2025b).
Valuable as they are, these concepts share two features that limit their purchase here: they
capture either discrete negative episodes or individual-level withdrawal, and they remain
conceptually separate from the value-creating trajectory from which they emerge.

We introduce co-creation attenuation to address this gap. We define it as the gradual,
cumulative weakening of co-creation across a community, in which users progressively withdraw
the resources that sustain their co-creator roles as access, infrastructure, relational,
normative and governance conditions fall out of alignment over time. So defined, attenuation
differs from adjacent concepts in three ways. First, whereas value co-destruction typically
denotes discrete, dyadic episodes of diminished well-being, attenuation is processual and
operates at the level of the ecosystem, unfolding without any single identifiable breakdown.
Second, whereas fatigue and disengagement are individual states, attenuation is a structural
property of the community, in which the erosion of conditions for some roles undermines the
value others can create. Third, rather than a sudden reversal, attenuation is the slow
downward phase of the same participation trajectory that earlier generated value, which is
why it can remain invisible until core contributors have already begun to leave. It is also
distinct from platform decay or declining community vitality: where those terms name the
system-level end-state of erosion, attenuation specifies the user-side process, the
progressive withdrawal of co-creating resources, through which that decline is produced.
Because embodiment and persistence intensify these dynamics, we finally specify the setting in
which we study them.

## 2.5 Social virtual worlds

Research on immersive environments uses "metaverse", "virtual reality" and "virtual world"
loosely, blurring conceptual boundaries (Dwivedi et al., 2023). For precision we adopt the
established construct of the social virtual world (SVW). A virtual world is a
computer-simulated, spatial environment in which users communicate through avatars; such
worlds divide into scripted, progression-based MMORPGs and emergent, socially oriented SVWs,
whose interactions and outcomes are open-ended and shaped by users rather than predetermined
(Schultze and Rennecker, 2007; Cowan and Ketron, 2019). By these criteria Light World is an
SVW rather than an MMORPG: it has no scripted progression, and its value arises from
open-ended exploration, user-generated worlds and social interaction. Whereas the SVWs that
anchor this literature, such as Second Life, are largely screen-based, Light World is accessed
through VR headsets, which adds embodied immersion and spatial co-presence (Belk, 2013;
Kozinets, 2023; Flavián et al., 2019); we therefore study a contemporary, VR-based SVW, a
setting whose perceived value and continuance dynamics are only beginning to be examined
(Yang and Han, 2021; Suh, 2024). As such it offers avatar mediation, spatial persistence and
user-generated worlds, but lacks the cross-platform interoperability and mature economic
infrastructure of fuller notions of the metaverse, a term we accordingly avoid.
