## 2.1 Value co-creation as resource integration

Service-dominant (S-D) logic provides the foundational premise for this study: value is not
embedded in outputs but is co-created in use and in context by actors who integrate their
resources (Vargo and Lusch, 2004, 2008). Rather than rehearsing this well-established
position, we foreground the mechanism that matters for online environments. Value emerges
when actors integrate operant resources (skills, knowledge, social ties, creativity) and
operand resources (tools, content, time) through the affordances a platform provides, within
institutional arrangements that coordinate their interaction (Lusch and Vargo, 2014; Vargo
et al., 2015; Ng and Vargo, 2018). Process accounts add that co-creation unfolds over time
through reciprocal encounters rather than single transactions (Payne et al., 2008; Grönroos,
2008).

This study therefore reads co-creation through a configurational lens: a given value outcome
is produced when specific actors integrate specific resources through specific platform
affordances. Stating the mechanism this way moves the analysis beyond the broad claim that
"users are resource integrators" and allows us to ask which resources, through which
affordances, by which actors, generate which forms of value, a question we carry into the
findings.

## 2.2 Forms of co-created value in online environments

Because co-creation generates heterogeneous outcomes, the value it produces must be
distinguished analytically rather than treated as a single undifferentiated good (Saarijärvi
et al., 2013; Alves et al., 2016). Synthesising work on online communities and digital
participation, we organise co-created value into five recurring forms that structure the
later analysis:

- **Experiential and aesthetic value**, the immersive, pleasurable and escapist worth of
  participating in and moving through online spaces (Schau et al., 2009);
- **Relational and emotional value**, the companionship, social support and sense of
  belonging generated through community ties (Algesheimer et al., 2005);
- **Informational and content value**, the user-generated content, guidance and knowledge
  that participation produces and circulates (Muniz and O'Guinn, 2001; Wasko and Faraj, 2005);
- **Symbolic and identity value**, the self-expression and recognition users derive from
  representing and distinguishing themselves online (Belk, 2013);
- **Normative and institutional value**, the order, safety and trust produced when users help
  define and enforce community rules (Kraut and Resnick, 2012).

Defining these dimensions in advance lets us specify, in the findings, how each cluster of
interaction activities generates particular forms of value, giving the analysis a cumulative
rather than merely descriptive structure.

## 2.3 Co-creator roles and participation trajectories in online communities

A long tradition in online community research shows that participation is unevenly
distributed and role-differentiated. Members range from silent lurkers to active posters and
core contributors, and the boundary between them is permeable (Nonnecke and Preece, 2000;
Preece et al., 2004; Sun et al., 2014). Whether members contribute depends on the resources
and incentives at play, such as reputation, reciprocity and a sense of community (Wasko and
Faraj, 2005; Blanchard and Markus, 2004; Ridings and Gefen, 2004). Value co-creation
research has translated these patterns into role typologies: assimilators, adaptors and
authenticators in sport fandom (Kolyperas et al., 2019); diverse contributor types on travel
platforms (Xu et al., 2025); and the movement of passive lurkers into active posting in
brand communities (Chapman and Dilmperi, 2022).

Across this work a common mechanism appears: roles are fluid rather than fixed, shaped by
participants' engagement intensity and resource contributions, so that peripheral members may
move towards the centre while highly engaged contributors may scale back (Dessart et al.,
2015; McColl-Kennedy et al., 2012). What remains underdeveloped is how such roles are
configured and how transitions occur when identity is enacted not through text and profiles
but through avatars, embodied co-presence and self-built worlds. Existing typologies also
treat roles largely as a static classification, leaving open how a single trajectory might
carry users from peripheral participation through deep contribution and, ultimately, towards
withdrawal.

## 2.4 From value co-destruction to co-creation attenuation

A growing body of work tempers the optimistic view of co-creation by examining its negative
counterpart. Value co-destruction refers to interactions in which actors' resource
integration diminishes rather than enhances well-being, typically arising from the
misintegration of resources or misaligned practices rather than a single catastrophic
failure (Plé and Chumpitaz Cáceres, 2010; Echeverri and Skålén, 2011, 2021). Related streams
document the dark side of engagement and failed co-creation (Heidenreich et al., 2015), the
disinhibition and toxic behaviour that corrode online interaction (Suler, 2004; Cheng et
al., 2017), and the fatigue, overload and exhaustion that drive disengagement and
discontinuance from social platforms (Maier et al., 2015; Ravindran et al., 2014; Nadeem et
al., 2025b). Yet these concepts largely capture either discrete negative episodes or
individual-level withdrawal, and they are rarely connected to the value-creating trajectory
from which they emerge.

We introduce **co-creation attenuation** to address this gap. We define it as *the gradual,
cumulative weakening of co-creation across a community, in which users progressively withdraw
the resources that sustain their co-creator roles as access, infrastructure, relational,
normative and governance conditions fall out of alignment over time*. So defined, attenuation
is distinct from adjacent concepts in three ways. First, whereas value co-destruction
typically denotes discrete, dyadic episodes of diminished well-being, attenuation is
processual and ecosystem-level, unfolding without any single identifiable breakdown. Second,
whereas fatigue and disengagement are individual states, attenuation is a structural property
of the community, in which the erosion of conditions for some roles undermines the value
others can create. Third, rather than a sudden reversal, attenuation is the slow downward
phase of the same participation trajectory that earlier generated value, which is why it can
remain invisible until core contributors have already begun to leave.

## 2.5 Immersive social VR platforms: terminology and boundary

Research on these environments uses "metaverse", "virtual reality", "virtual world" and
"virtual community" loosely and often interchangeably, blurring conceptual boundaries
(Dwivedi et al., 2023). For clarity we adopt the term **social VR platform** throughout, and
treat it as an immersive virtual community: a persistent, three-dimensional, avatar-mediated
space accessed through VR hardware, in which co-present users communicate through spatial
audio and gesture and produce, share and inhabit user-generated worlds (Belk, 2013; Kozinets,
2023; Flavián et al., 2019). This setting differs from text- and screen-based communities in
its embodied co-presence, spatial persistence and avatar-based identity, properties that
deepen immersion and emotional investment.

At the same time, we state the boundary of the case explicitly. The platform we study, Light
World, exhibits some metaverse characteristics, namely high immersion, spatial persistence,
avatar identity and user-generated worlds, but lacks others often attributed to the
metaverse, such as cross-platform interoperability and a mature economic infrastructure. We
therefore make no claim about the metaverse in general, and confine our analysis to value
co-creation and attenuation within an immersive social VR community.
